What treatment-resistant depression means

If you are in crisis, call or text 988. In Utah you can also use SafeUT. If it is an emergency, call 911. This website is not monitored for emergencies. More options are on the crisis page.

People use "treatment-resistant" in a lot of ways. The National Institute of Mental Health uses a specific one: treatment-resistant depression is when a person does not get better after trying at least two antidepressants. (NIMH: Depression)

That definition is about antidepressant trials. It is not a statement that therapy failed, and it is not a statement that nothing else can help.

What NIMH says comes next

NIMH describes depression treatment as psychotherapy, medication, or both. The choice depends on the person, their preferences, and their medical situation, and it is made with a clinician. Finding a fit can take more than one try.

On medication, NIMH says:

  • Antidepressants usually need 4 to 8 weeks. Problems with sleep, appetite, and concentration often ease before mood does.
  • One option after two antidepressants have not helped is esketamine, a nasal spray given in a doctor's office, clinic, or hospital. People often keep taking an antidepressant pill along with it.
  • Another option is adding a different kind of medicine, such as an antipsychotic or an anticonvulsant, to an antidepressant.
  • Brain stimulation, including ECT and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, is generally used after psychotherapy and medication have not been enough. Those therapies usually continue alongside it. NIMH says it is sometimes considered sooner when depression is life-threatening, such as when a person has stopped eating or drinking or is at a high risk of suicide.

Do not start, stop, or change a medicine based on this page. Talk with the prescriber. Open Book has not said it provides esketamine, ECT, or transcranial magnetic stimulation. For some medications, an in-person visit may be required.

NIMH also notes an FDA warning: some children, teens, and young adults under 25 may have an increase in suicidal thoughts or behavior when taking antidepressants, especially in the first few weeks or after a dose change. People of any age should be watched closely in that window.

The two studies already cited on the depression page are about therapy plus medication for major depression and for chronic depression. They are not studies of PTSD or GAD, and this page does not use them that way.

When depression hasn't lifted yet

If you've tried more than one antidepressant and still feel stuck, you're not alone, and it isn't a personal failing. Clinicians often call this treatment-resistant depression. It's common, and there are still real next steps.

Research shows that for many adults whose depression hasn't improved enough on medication, adding structured therapy helps. At Open Book, your counselor and Steffani Winter, MSN, APRN, PMHNP-BC, can work as one team when you agree to it. Together we review what you've already tried, adjust medication when it makes sense, and check in on your symptoms regularly so the plan changes when something isn't working. If another kind of treatment might help, we'll talk through options with you and coordinate with your other providers.
