How medication management and therapy work together

Editor's note: the research sentences below are only the Cuijpers 2014 and Keller 2000 wording already on the medication page. Those sentences name major depression, panic disorder, OCD, and chronic depression. This draft does not extend them to PTSD or GAD. It also does not say that a controlled medication will be prescribed over telehealth.

Open Book Counseling offers psychiatric medication management coordinated with therapy when therapy is part of your care. The published line is: mental health medication, working hand in hand with your therapy.

Appointments are with Steffani Winter, MSN, APRN, PMHNP-BC, a board-certified psychiatric mental health nurse practitioner. She is a nurse practitioner, not a psychiatrist or a physician. Only she prescribes medication at Open Book. The counselors, including Scott Frazier, CMHC, CCTP, do not prescribe.

You do not need to be a therapy client here to meet with her. If you already have a therapist here, or somewhere else, she can work alongside that care. You can also keep a therapist you already have outside the practice.

Two kinds of help

The medication page says medication and therapy help in different ways. Medication can ease symptoms like low mood, panic, or poor sleep, which makes it easier to do the work of therapy. Therapy builds the skills, insight, and healing that last after treatment ends.

Medication can be one part of a treatment plan. It is not a cure-all, and it is often most useful when it is paired with therapy and other supports. The page also says therapy alone can be a reasonable choice, and that Steffani will talk through options, possible side effects, and alternatives. Medication isn't right for everyone.

At Open Book, Steffani and your therapist work as one team when you are seen by both. With your permission, they share a plan so your medication and therapy support each other, including when you're stepping down from a hospital stay. If you prefer to stay with your current prescriber, she will not require you to switch. If you ask about transferring care, she still completes her own evaluation before making changes or taking over prescriptions.

What the page cites

Research supports using them together, in the conditions those two papers name.

A review of 52 randomized trials with more than 3,600 adults found that adding therapy to antidepressant medication worked meaningfully better than medication alone for major depression, panic disorder, and OCD, and the benefit lasted up to two years (Cuijpers et al., World Psychiatry, 2014).

In a large trial of adults with chronic depression, 73% improved with medication plus therapy, compared with 48% with either one alone (Keller et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2000).

Those are the findings as the site states them. They are about major depression, panic disorder, OCD, and chronic depression in adults.

The page is general information, not medical advice. Decisions about care belong in a visit with the prescriber. Do not start, stop, or change a medication based on this draft or the website.

What a course of care includes

Psychiatric medication management is ongoing care with a licensed prescriber. It is not a one-time visit for a refill. The page lists a typical course:

  • A psychiatric evaluation of your symptoms, health history, current medications, and goals.
  • A conversation about whether medication is appropriate.
  • A prescription, when you and the prescriber decide to move forward.
  • Follow-up visits to see how the medication is working, watch for side effects, and adjust the dose or change medications if needed.

People respond to medications in different ways. The first option is not always the right fit, and it can take time to judge how a medicine is working. Many people are seen more often while a medication is being started or adjusted, and less often once things are stable. The schedule is set with you.

Steffani sees children, teens, and adults, starting at age 8. Now accepting appointments. She sees people in the Draper and Bountiful offices and by telehealth. For some medications, an in-person visit may be required. She will explain what applies in your situation.

She can prescribe controlled medications when clinically appropriate, including ADHD medications. Whether a particular medicine is a good fit depends on a clinical evaluation and applicable rules.

The practice is credentialing Steffani with the same insurance plans it accepts for therapy. Credentialing is in progress, so confirm coverage before you come in. Call (385) 524-6665. Plan details are on fees and insurance.

You do not need a referral. Request an appointment, or call. You will usually speak first with Makayla Jones, the admin manager. Please don't send health details by email. Asking for a time does not confirm an appointment.
