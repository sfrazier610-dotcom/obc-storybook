# The first 30 days after a psychiatric hospital stay

Unpublished draft. Not linked from the site.

If you are in crisis, call or text [988](tel:988) (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, 24/7). In Utah you can also use [SafeUT](https://safeut.org/). If it is an emergency, call [911](tel:911). This website is not monitored for emergencies. More options are on the [crisis page](/crisis/).

Coming home after a psychiatric hospital stay is a health event. The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention says people need time, rest, and support while they recover, the same way they would after any other serious health concern. You do not have to have the whole story figured out before you start getting better. ([After an attempt](https://afsp.org/after-an-attempt))

## The first days at home

A few ordinary things help more than they look.

- Be gentle with yourself. You just got through a life-threatening health crisis.
- Sleep, meals, and a little movement matter. AFSP names exercise, food, sleep, and time with people who are good for you.
- Keep the follow-up appointments that were set before you left.
- Take medication the way it was prescribed. The National Institute of Mental Health says not to start, stop, or change a medicine on your own, and that many antidepressants need about 4 to 8 weeks before you can tell how they are working. Sleep, appetite, and concentration often improve before mood does. ([NIMH: Depression](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))
- Tell one or two people you trust what you want them to do if things get hard again.

NIMH also says that some children, teens, and young adults under 25 can have an increase in suicidal thoughts or behavior when they start an antidepressant or the dose changes, especially in the first few weeks. The FDA advises that people of any age be watched closely in those weeks. If that happens, contact the prescriber, call or text 988, or call 911 if someone is in immediate danger.

A written safety plan belongs in this stretch of time. The steps are on [Safety planning, step by step](safety-planning-step-by-step.md). A printable sheet is the [SAMHSA 988 safety plan](https://www.samhsa.gov/sites/default/files/988-safety-plan.pdf) (updated April 22, 2025).

## If you are coming to Open Book

Scott Frazier, CMHC, CCTP, and Steffani Winter, MSN, APRN, PMHNP-BC, work together with clients who are leaving the hospital after trauma or a suicide attempt. Scott provides the therapy. Steffani provides the medication management. Steffani is a nurse practitioner. She is not a psychiatrist or a physician. Only she prescribes at Open Book.

Hospitals and other providers can use the [referral page](/professional/referrals/). It helps to send a discharge summary, a current medication list, and a signed release so the therapist and Steffani can share a plan with you. You can also [request an appointment](/professional/intake.html) yourself. Please don’t put health details in an email. Asking for a time does not confirm the appointment.

Care for depression, including suicidal thoughts, is described on the [depression page](/professional/services/depression-suicidal-thoughts.html). Medication management is described on its [own page](/professional/services/medication-management.html).
